On **26 November 2014**, UNESCO added the **roda de capoeira** to the Representative List of the **Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity**. For an art that had been a crime in Brazil less than a century earlier, it was a historic moment. Here's what was recognised, how it came about and what it means for capoeiristas everywhere, including here in Berlin.

<!--more-->

## What is intangible cultural heritage?

Most people know UNESCO's World Heritage list of places, like old towns, temples and national parks. Since 2003, UNESCO also protects **intangible** heritage: living traditions such as music, dance, rituals, festivals and crafts, which only exist as long as people keep practising them and passing them on.

## From a crime to a heritage

Capoeira's road to UNESCO was long:

- **1890**: Brazil's new penal code made capoeira a crime. Capoeiristas could be arrested and sent to prison.
- **1930s**: Mestre Bimba and Mestre Pastinha opened capoeira schools in Salvador, and capoeira slowly gained respect. Read about them in [Who was Mestre Bimba?]({{< relref "/capoeira/mestre-bimba" >}}) and [Who was Mestre Pastinha?]({{< relref "/capoeira/mestre-pastinha" >}})
- **1940**: the new penal code no longer listed capoeira as a crime.
- **2008**: Brazil's national heritage institute, **IPHAN**, registered two things as Brazilian cultural heritage: the **roda de capoeira** and the **ofício dos mestres de capoeira**, the knowledge and work of capoeira masters.
- **2014**: at its meeting in Paris, UNESCO's committee inscribed the roda de capoeira on its Representative List.

You can read the full story in [The History of Capoeira]({{< relref "/capoeira/history-of-capoeira" >}}).

## What exactly was recognised?

UNESCO recognised the **roda**: the circle in which capoeira is played, sung and celebrated. In its description, the roda is a place where:

- people of different ages and backgrounds play together, through movement, music and song;
- knowledge is passed on orally, from mestres to students, in the songs and the rituals;
- the memory of resistance to slavery is kept alive;
- respect, friendship and community are practised.

In other words, it's not only the kicks and the acrobatics that count, but the whole roda: the berimbau, the songs, the rituals and the relationship between mestres and students.

## Why it matters

The UNESCO listing doesn't make capoeira more "official" or change how anyone plays. But it matters in several ways:

- **Recognition**: it says to the world that capoeira, born among enslaved Africans and their descendants, is part of the heritage of all humanity.
- **Safeguarding**: Brazil committed to protect the tradition, for example with support for older mestres, research and documentation.
- **Pride**: for capoeiristas, especially in Brazil, it was a celebration of the generations of mestres who kept capoeira alive when it was banned.

Capoeira isn't the only Afro-Brazilian tradition on the list. **Samba de roda** from the Recôncavo Baiano was recognised years earlier: read [What is Samba de Roda?]({{< relref "/capoeira/samba-de-roda" >}})
