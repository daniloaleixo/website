In Bahia, a Catholic saint and an orixá can share a feast day, a colour and even a song. Every January, women dressed in white wash the steps of a Catholic church in Salvador with scented water, in honour of both Jesus and Oxalá. This blending of religions is called **syncretism**, and it's everywhere in Afro-Brazilian culture, capoeira included. Here's where it comes from and what it means.

<!--more-->

## What is syncretism?

**Syncretism** is the mixing or linking of different religious traditions. In Brazil the word usually describes how the **orixás**, the divine forces of Afro-Brazilian religions like Candomblé, came to be associated with **Catholic saints**. If you don't know the orixás yet, start with [Candomblé and Capoeira]({{< relref "/capoeira/candomble-and-capoeira" >}}).

## How it happened

During slavery, the Catholic Church was the official religion of colonial Brazil. Enslaved Africans were baptised and expected to worship the saints, while their own religions were forbidden or treated with suspicion.

A common explanation is that they hid their orixás behind the saints: praying in front of a statue of Saint Barbara, for example, while honouring Iansã. Historians add that it wasn't only a disguise. Catholic brotherhoods (*irmandades*) of Black people were among the few places where Africans and their descendants could meet, organise and celebrate, and over generations many people sincerely took part in both traditions. The links between saints and orixás were often based on real similarities: a warrior saint for a warrior orixá, a saint of the sea for the queen of the sea.

## Orixás and their saints

The pairs differ from region to region. These are some of the most common ones in **Bahia**:

| Orixá | Linked to | Saint |
|---|---|---|
| Oxalá | creation, peace, white | Senhor do Bonfim (Jesus) |
| Iemanjá | the sea, motherhood | Nossa Senhora da Conceição |
| Ogum | iron, roads, battle | Santo Antônio |
| Oxóssi | the hunt, the forest | São Jorge |
| Iansã | wind, storms | Santa Bárbara |
| Xangô | thunder, justice | São Jerônimo |
| Omolu / Obaluaê | illness and healing | São Lázaro, São Roque |
| Ibeji | the twins | São Cosme and São Damião |

In Rio de Janeiro, for example, Ogum is linked to São Jorge and Oxóssi to São Sebastião instead.

## The Lavagem do Bonfim

The best-known symbol of syncretism in Bahia is the **Lavagem do Bonfim**, the "washing of Bonfim". Every year on the second Thursday of January, a huge procession walks about eight kilometres from the Conceição da Praia church in Salvador's lower city to the **Church of Senhor do Bonfim**. At the front go the **baianas**, women in white dresses carrying jugs of scented water (*água de cheiro*) and flowers. At the church they wash the steps and the courtyard, while the crowd sings and celebrates.

For Catholics it's a feast for the Senhor do Bonfim, Jesus. For many followers of Candomblé it's also a celebration of **Oxalá**, whose colour is white. Both are present at the same time.

## Not everyone agrees

Syncretism is also debated. In **1983**, a group of leading priestesses (*ialorixás*) of Salvador's oldest terreiros, among them **Mãe Stella de Oxóssi**, published a statement rejecting syncretism. They argued that it came from a time of oppression, and that Candomblé is a religion in its own right, which doesn't need Catholic saints to be accepted. Others see syncretism as part of their faith and identity. Both views are part of Bahia today.

## Syncretism in capoeira

Capoeira grew up in the same world, and you can hear it:

- In the **louvação**, the call that follows the opening song of a roda, players sing *"Iê, viva meu Deus"*: praise to God, in a ritual with African roots.
- The famous toques **São Bento Grande** and **São Bento Pequeno** carry the name of a Catholic saint, Saint Benedict.
- Many songs mention **Santa Bárbara**, **Nossa Senhora** or the **Senhor do Bonfim**, and for many singers these names also carry the memory of the orixás.
- Some capoeiristas cross themselves at the foot of the berimbau before they play, a Catholic gesture inside an Afro-Brazilian ritual.

Like the songs about Iemanjá and Ogum, these are part of the folklore that capoeira carries with it. You don't have to be religious to sing them, but knowing what they mean makes the roda richer.
